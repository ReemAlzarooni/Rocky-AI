Rocket_Project_Information.docx supplied by the AquaNova team.

PURPOSE AND COMPETITION - Team name: AquaNova. - Chatbot name: Rocky
AI. - Project goal: design, build, test, and optimise a water rocket for
horizontal distance. - Competition length limit: less than 50 cm,
measured from nozzle to nose tip. - Qualifying distance: at least 6 m. -
Awards include the top three distances and a Best-Designed Rocket
award. - The Best-Designed award considers creativity, engineering,
appearance, innovation, materials, and sustainability. - The competition
launch angle specified in the project brief is 45 degrees. - Competition
day listed in the team’s document: Sunday, 15 November 2026.

HOW A WATER ROCKET WORKS - A water rocket is a plastic bottle partly
filled with water and pressurised air. - At release, compressed air
pushes water out through the nozzle. The backward-moving water and
forward reaction of the rocket illustrate Newton’s third law. - The
flight can be described in stages: pressurisation, water-thrust phase, a
short remaining-air pulse, and coasting under gravity and air
resistance. - The team’s report explains that the water provides
reaction mass and compressed air stores energy. - During coasting,
gravity and air drag shape the projectile trajectory. - The centre of
gravity (CG) and centre of pressure (CP) are important to aerodynamic
stability. The report explains that the CG should be ahead of the CP for
a restoring aerodynamic effect. - Fins help move the centre of pressure
toward the rear and help the rocket maintain a stable attitude. - Air
resistance reduces the actual range compared with an ideal
projectile-motion calculation.

AQUANOVA’S ROCKET — MEASURED DESIGN DETAILS - Body: clear, slightly
green-tinted 2.5 L Sprite PET soft-drink bottle. - Bottle diameter:
108.2 mm (measured circumference: 34 cm). - Bottle height, including
nozzle: 37 cm. - Total rocket length: 48.5 cm, within the stated 50 cm
limit. - Nose cone: black, 3D-printed PLA, tangent-ogive shape,
slip-on. - Fin assembly: black, 3D-printed PLA fin can with four swept
fins on a slip-on ring. - The nose cone and fin assembly slide onto the
bottle; the report says this arrangement was chosen to allow parts to be
swapped during prototyping. - Dry mass without water: 237 g
(measured). - Water amount recorded for the team’s design: 0.85 L (850
g). - Launch mass recorded: 1,087 g (measured). - Empty-rocket CG: 24 cm
from the nozzle. - CP: 20 cm from the nozzle. - Recorded CG–CP
separation: 4 cm, or 0.37 caliber; the team’s document defines one
caliber as the 10.8 cm body diameter. - Launch angle in the project
brief/design record: 45 degrees. - Trial flight recorded by the team: 58
m, with little to no spin.

MATERIALS - Recyclable 2.5 L PET soda bottle for the body. - PLA
3D-printing filament for the nose cone and fin can. - Duct tape. -
Superglue. - The report describes the nose cone and fin can as reusable
parts that can be transferred to a replacement bottle.

DESIGN PROCESS AND ANALYSIS - The team researched nose-cone and fin
concepts before using AI-assisted modelling and simulations. - The
selected concepts were an ogive-shaped nose cone and swept-back rear
fins. - The team used its own bottle measurements and a product photo as
inputs to its modelling work. - The report says the bottle was modelled
in 3D and the nose cone and fins were made as parametric 3D models in
Python. - Stability was analysed using the Barrowman method and
cross-checked using a cardboard-cutout/lateral-area method. - The team’s
flight simulations considered water thrust, expansion of the air as
water leaves, a remaining-air pulse, air drag, and gravity. - The team
compared design concepts in simulations and cross-checked its work using
NASA Glenn Research Center’s RocketModeler III in bottle-rocket mode. -
The team’s document notes that the NASA simulator used a higher drag
assumption than its own estimate, so the team treated predicted range as
a range of possible outcomes rather than a single certain value. - The
team reports that its chosen design’s simulated range was close to its
58 m trial-flight result. The report also notes that the trial
conditions were not identical to the simulation conditions.

DESIGN RATIONALE — AS REPORTED BY THE TEAM - The 2.5 L bottle was
selected after comparing bottle options in the team’s simulations. - The
nose cone has a smooth tangent-ogive profile. Its tip is rounded rather
than sharp. - Four swept fins were selected to provide stability for the
team’s relatively short, wide rocket. - The team selected a fin size it
considered sufficient for stability without unnecessary added drag and
mass. - The nose cone and fin can use a slip-on arrangement to make
swapping components during prototyping easier. - The team chose not to
add nose ballast, prioritising range while accepting a smaller static
stability margin than the general guideline discussed in its report. -
The report states that the trial rocket left the launcher cleanly and
flew with little to no spin.

WATER AMOUNT — TEAM RECORD - The team’s recorded water amount is 0.85 L
(850 g). - The report says this amount was selected based on the team’s
simulations and weighed so that the amount would be consistent between
trials. - Rocky should report this as the team’s documented design
choice, not recommend or calculate pressure settings or launch
adjustments.

SUSTAINABILITY - The main body is a recyclable PET soda bottle. - The
nose cone and fin can are reusable and can be moved to another bottle. -
The team describes PLA as a plant-based plastic made from renewable
feedstocks; its report notes that industrial composting conditions are
required for composting. - The report emphasises using material
selectively and avoiding unnecessary added ballast. - Water is the
propellant; the report says no fuel is burned and no chemicals are
released during operation.

SAFETY — IMPORTANT - The team’s report specifies safety goggles during
authorised pressurising and launching activities. - The report says
everyone should remain behind the designated safety line once the rocket
is pressurised. - Never approach, touch, or handle a pressurised
rocket. - Only authorised people should operate the pump and launcher. -
The bottle should be inspected by the responsible supervisor before each
authorised launch. The report says not to use bottles with cracks,
dents, scratches, or whitening, and to retire bottles after repeated
use. - The report specifies a rounded nose tip as a safer design feature
than a sharp point. - Rocky must not provide instructions for
pressurising, constructing, modifying, or launching a pressurised
rocket, pressure targets, launch optimisation, or troubleshooting
pressurised equipment. For operational questions, Rocky should direct
users to the course instructor and the authorised launch supervisor and
repeat the applicable safety rules.
